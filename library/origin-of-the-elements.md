# Where the elements come from

Every atom heavier than helium was made in a star or in the death of one. The periodic table can be read as a map of astrophysical events: a few minutes of the early universe, the slow burning of stars, the collapse of massive ones, the detonation of white dwarfs and the collision of neutron stars. This document takes each source in turn and says how we know.

## The first three minutes

The Big Bang made hydrogen, helium and a trace of lithium, and then stopped. Within about twenty minutes the universe had cooled and thinned too far for fusion to continue. The mix it left, roughly three quarters hydrogen and one quarter helium by mass, is still the bulk composition of the universe.

### Why it stopped at helium

Building heavier nuclei by adding one proton or neutron at a time runs into a gap: no stable nucleus has five or eight particles. Helium-4 plus a proton falls apart, and so do two helium-4 nuclei stuck together. Crossing the gap needs three helium nuclei to meet almost at once, which requires density and time. The expanding universe had neither.

^ "no stable nucleus has five or eight particles" note elaborate: Beryllium-8, the product of two helium-4 nuclei, survives for about a ten-millionth of a billionth of a second. That is long enough to matter inside a dense star and far too short in the thinning early universe.

### How we know

The predicted amounts of helium and deuterium depend on one number, the ratio of ordinary matter to light. The oldest gas clouds, seen by the light they absorb from quasars behind them, contain deuterium at the predicted level, and the same matter density is measured independently from the cosmic microwave background. Two methods that share no instruments agree.

^ "deuterium" gloss define: hydrogen with one extra neutron
^ "Two methods that share no instruments agree" note challenge: Lithium does not. Old stars show about a third of the lithium-7 the theory predicts. Whether the stars destroyed it or the theory is missing something is unsettled.

## Fusion in ordinary stars

Stars are held up by the heat of fusion in their cores. For most of their lives they turn hydrogen into helium; when the hydrogen runs out the core contracts, heats, and starts burning helium into carbon and oxygen. A star like the Sun stops there. Carbon, nitrogen and much of the oxygen in living things came from stars of this modest kind.

### Hydrogen burning

In the Sun's core four protons become one helium nucleus, and the 0.7 percent of mass that goes missing leaves as energy. The first step is absurdly slow: two protons must collide and, during the collision, one must turn into a neutron through the weak force. A given proton waits billions of years for this. The slowness is why the Sun lasts.

^ "one must turn into a neutron through the weak force" note why: Two protons alone do not bind. Only a proton and a neutron do, so fusion has to wait for a rare weak decay to happen in the instant of collision.

### The triple-alpha process

Helium burning makes carbon by fusing three helium nuclei. Two form unstable beryllium-8, and before it decays a third must arrive. The rate would be hopelessly low except that carbon-12 has an excited state at almost exactly the energy of beryllium plus helium. Fred Hoyle predicted that state in 1953 from the bare fact that carbon exists, and experimenters at Caltech found it where he said.

^ "an excited state at almost exactly the energy" note elaborate: It is called the Hoyle state and lies 7.65 million electron volts above carbon's ground state. A resonance means the incoming particles fit an available state of the product, which raises the reaction rate by many orders of magnitude.

### How the material gets out

A star that fuses elements and keeps them is no use to anyone. Sun-like stars end as red giants whose outer layers, enriched by material dredged up from the interior, drift away as slow winds and glowing shells. The core left behind is a white dwarf of carbon and oxygen.

## Massive stars

Stars above about eight solar masses keep going. Their cores burn carbon, neon, oxygen and silicon in turn, each stage hotter and shorter than the last, until the centre is iron. Iron is the end of the line for fusion, and the collapse that follows scatters the star's layers into space.

### The onion

Late in its life a massive star is layered: hydrogen outside, then helium, carbon, oxygen, silicon, and an iron core. Each fuel is the ash of the one above it. The stages speed up violently. Hydrogen lasts millions of years; silicon burning lasts about a day.

^ "silicon burning lasts about a day" note why: Each stage releases less energy per reaction than the one before, and at these temperatures most of what is released leaves at once as neutrinos, so fuel must be consumed ever faster to hold the core up.

### Why iron is the end

Nuclei near iron and nickel are the most tightly bound. Fusing lighter nuclei toward iron releases energy; fusing iron into something heavier costs energy. An iron core therefore has no way to pay for its own support. When it grows past about 1.4 solar masses it collapses in under a second to a neutron star.

^ "most tightly bound" note elaborate: The record for binding energy per particle belongs to nickel-62, with iron-58 and iron-56 close behind. Iron-56 is the common one because of how silicon burning proceeds, not because it sits at the exact peak.

### Core collapse

The collapse halts when the core reaches nuclear density, and the rebound, helped by a flood of neutrinos, blows the outer layers away. The explosion delivers the star's oxygen, neon, magnesium, silicon and sulphur to interstellar space. Most of the oxygen you breathe was made this way.

^ "helped by a flood of neutrinos" note elaborate: About 99 percent of the energy of a core-collapse supernova leaves as neutrinos. Detectors caught some two dozen of them from Supernova 1987A, a few hours before its light arrived.

## Exploding white dwarfs

A white dwarf that gains mass from a companion star can ignite all at once. Its carbon and oxygen fuse in seconds to elements near iron, and the star is destroyed. These explosions, called Type Ia supernovae, are the main source of the iron in your blood.

### The explosion

A white dwarf is held up by the pressure of crowded electrons, not by heat, so it has no thermostat. When fusion begins, the star does not expand and cool as an ordinary star would. The burning runs away and consumes the star, producing about half a solar mass of radioactive nickel-56.

^ "it has no thermostat" note define: In an ordinary star, extra heat raises the pressure, the gas expands and cools, and fusion slows. Electron crowding pressure does not depend on temperature, so that feedback is missing.

### Reading the light

The nickel decays to cobalt and then to iron, with half-lives of six and seventy-seven days. The fading of the supernova follows those clocks, which is direct evidence of what was made. Because these explosions are nearly alike in brightness, they also serve as distance markers, and it was their unexpected faintness at great distances that revealed in 1998 that the expansion of the universe is speeding up.

## Beyond iron

About half the elements heavier than iron are made by slow neutron capture in old giant stars, and the other half by rapid capture in events violent enough to supply neutrons by the trillion. For the rapid kind, the one site confirmed by direct observation is the merger of two neutron stars.

### The slow process

Neutrons have no charge, so a nucleus cannot repel them. In aging giant stars a trickle of free neutrons lets nuclei gain one every few decades or centuries, with time to decay between captures. The path walks up the table through stable nuclei and makes much of the barium, strontium and lead.

^ "with time to decay between captures" note define: After capturing a neutron a nucleus is often unstable and converts a neutron to a proton, moving one place up the periodic table. Slow means this decay nearly always happens before the next capture.

### The rapid process

Where neutrons are dense enough, a nucleus swallows dozens in under a second, far faster than it can decay, and is driven out to extremely neutron-rich forms. When the flood stops these decay back toward stability and land on gold, platinum, uranium and the other heavy elements the slow path cannot reach.

^ "the slow path cannot reach" note why: Above bismuth every nucleus is radioactive and many decay in seconds, so a nucleus gaining one neutron every few decades falls back before it can climb. Uranium and thorium can only be reached by outrunning the decays.

### A collision observed

On 17 August 2017 gravitational-wave detectors recorded two neutron stars spiralling together, and telescopes found the glow that followed in a galaxy 130 million light years away. The light reddened over days in the way predicted for freshly made heavy elements absorbing it. Estimates put the yield at several Earth masses of gold.

^ "17 August 2017" gloss elaborate: the event known as GW170817
^ "Estimates put the yield at several Earth masses of gold" note challenge: Whether mergers make enough to explain all of the heavy elements, or whether rare kinds of supernova contribute, is still argued. Old stars rich in such elements seem to need a source that acted very early.

## Reading the table

Put together, each element has a ledger of sources, and the atoms in a human body have mixed origins. The ledger is checked against the spectra of stars of different ages, which record what the galaxy contained when each one formed.

### A body's inventory

The hydrogen in your water is thirteen billion years old and has never been inside a star's core. Your carbon and nitrogen came mostly from the winds of dying sun-like stars, your oxygen from massive stars that exploded, your iron largely from white dwarfs, and the iodine in your thyroid from colliding neutron stars or something equally violent.

### Fossils in old stars

A star's surface keeps the composition of the gas it formed from. The oldest stars in the galaxy have less than a thousandth of the Sun's share of iron, and their ratios of one element to another differ from the Sun's in ways that show which sources switched on first: massive stars within millions of years, white dwarfs and slow capture only after a billion or so.

^ "which sources switched on first" note example: Old stars are rich in oxygen relative to iron. Oxygen comes from massive stars, which die young, and most iron from white dwarfs, which take much longer to explode, so early gas had the first without much of the second.
