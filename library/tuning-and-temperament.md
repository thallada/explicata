# Tuning and temperament

No keyboard can be tuned so that every interval is pure. This is arithmetic, not a failure of craft: the simple frequency ratios that sound smooth cannot all hold at once among twelve fixed notes. Every tuning system in Western music is a decision about where to put the error, and the piano's answer, to spread it evenly, makes every key usable and almost every interval slightly wrong.

## Why some intervals sound smooth

Two notes sound smooth together when their frequencies stand in a simple ratio such as 2 to 1 or 3 to 2. The cause lies in the overtones of each note: with simple ratios they line up, and with ratios that are slightly off they beat against each other.

### Overtones

A plucked string vibrates as a whole, and also in halves, thirds, quarters and so on, all at once. The note we hear is a stack of frequencies at one, two, three, four times the lowest. Pitch comes from the lowest; the colour of the instrument comes from the balance of the rest.

^ "all at once" note example: Touch a guitar string lightly at its midpoint and pluck. The whole-string motion is stopped and the half-string motion remains, sounding an octave higher. It was there all along.

### Simple ratios

When two notes are a fifth apart, in the ratio 3 to 2, every second overtone of the upper note lands exactly on every third overtone of the lower. The two sounds fuse. The purest intervals are the octave at 2 to 1, the fifth at 3 to 2, the fourth at 4 to 3 and the major third at 5 to 4.

### Beats

Two frequencies that nearly coincide produce a slow throbbing at the difference between them. If a fifth is slightly out of tune, the overtones that should coincide beat. Tuners count these beats; a tempered fifth near the middle of a piano beats a little less than once a second.

^ "a slow throbbing at the difference between them" note example: Notes at 440 and 442 cycles a second, sounded together, swell and fade twice a second.

## The comma

Tune upward by twelve pure fifths and you ought to arrive at the note you started on, seven octaves higher. You overshoot by about a quarter of a semitone. That gap is the Pythagorean comma, and a second mismatch, between fifths and thirds, is just as stubborn. Both follow from the fact that powers of two, three and five never coincide.

### Twelve fifths against seven octaves

Twelve fifths multiply the frequency by 3/2 twelve times, which is about 129.75. Seven octaves multiply it by 128. The ratio between them is about 1.0136. No number of fifths ever equals a number of octaves, because a power of three is odd and a power of two is even.

^ "about 1.0136" gloss elaborate: 23.5 cents, where a cent is a hundredth of an equal-tempered semitone
^ "No number of fifths ever equals a number of octaves" note elaborate: Twelve is used because it is a near miss. The next closer approximations come at 41 and 53 fifths, and instruments with 53 notes to the octave have been built.

### Thirds against fifths

Four fifths up from C, less two octaves, gives an E. A pure major third above C also gives an E. They differ: the first is 81/64 above C and the second is 5/4, which is 80/64. The stacked-fifths third is sharp by the ratio 81 to 80, called the syntonic comma, and it sounds restless where the pure third sounds calm.

^ "syntonic comma" gloss define: 21.5 cents, about a fifth of a semitone

### No way out

A system with fixed pitches would need pure octaves, pure fifths and pure thirds together. Those are built from the primes two, three and five, and no product of powers of different primes equals another. Something must give. Singers and string players adjust each chord by ear as they go; a keyboard must choose once.

^ "adjust each chord by ear as they go" note challenge: Even that fails over time. A simple four-chord sequence sung with every interval pure ends about a fifth of a semitone lower than it began, and repeating it sinks the pitch further. Good choirs make small compromises to stay put.

## Tunings that choose

Before equal temperament, tuners put the error where the music would not go. Pythagorean tuning kept the fifths pure and let the thirds suffer; meantone kept the thirds sweet and paid with one unusable fifth and several unusable keys.

### Pythagorean

Tune eleven fifths pure and the twelfth must absorb the whole comma. It is narrow by a quarter of a semitone and howls. Medieval music, built on fifths and fourths and treating the third as a dissonance, suited this tuning well.

^ "howls" gloss define: such an interval is called a wolf

### Meantone

When thirds became the heart of harmony in the Renaissance, tuners narrowed each fifth slightly, by a quarter of the syntonic comma, so that four of them stack to a pure third. Eight major thirds come out pure and the common keys sound sweeter than anything on a modern piano. The debt collects in one wolf fifth and four thirds so wide they are useless.

^ "four thirds so wide they are useless" note elaborate: In quarter-comma meantone, G sharp and A flat are different pitches about two fifths of a semitone apart. A keyboard has one key for both and must pick. Some organs were built with split black keys to offer both.

### The cost

In meantone a piece can move among perhaps six major keys. Past that the wolf appears. Composers wrote within the fence, and sometimes used the rough keys on purpose for moments of pain.

## Spreading it out

The later answer was to share the error so that no key is unusable. Well temperaments shared it unevenly, giving each key a distinct flavour; equal temperament shares it exactly evenly, making every key identical.

### Well temperament

In the late seventeenth century tuners such as Andreas Werckmeister described schemes that narrow some fifths more than others so that every key can be played. Keys with few sharps or flats have calm thirds; remote keys have bright, tense ones. Moving between keys changes the colour, and composers counted on that.

### What Bach meant

*The Well-Tempered Clavier*, two books of preludes and fugues in all twenty-four keys, shows that a single tuning can serve them all. The title does not say equal-tempered, and most scholars now think Bach used an unequal scheme of his own in which the keys differ in character.

^ "an unequal scheme of his own" note challenge: Nobody knows which. One proposal reads the row of loops drawn at the top of the title page as a tuning recipe. It is ingenious and unproven.

### Equal temperament

Divide the octave into twelve equal steps, each a frequency ratio of the twelfth root of two, about 1.0595. Every fifth is narrow by a fiftieth of a semitone, which hardly anyone hears. Every major third is wide by about a seventh of a semitone, which anyone can hear beside a pure one. The scheme was calculated in the 1580s by Zhu Zaiyu in China and by Simon Stevin in Flanders, within a few years of each other.

^ "wide by about a seventh of a semitone" note example: On a piano the third C to E beats about ten times a second in the middle register. We are so used to it that a pure third can at first sound flat.

## Living with it

Equal temperament became the standard for pianos during the nineteenth century, and with it came music that treats all twelve notes as equals. The older tunings are still used where their strengths count, and in practice even the piano is not tuned to the formula.

### What was gained and lost

Free movement between any keys made possible the harmony of Chopin, Wagner and jazz. What was lost is the difference between keys and the stillness of pure thirds. Barbershop quartets and good string quartets still find those thirds by ear, and the chords seem to ring.

^ "the chords seem to ring" note why: When intervals are pure, the overtones of the separate voices coincide and reinforce one another, and the ear can pick out a high tone that no one is singing.

### Stretched octaves

A real piano string is stiff, and stiffness raises its overtones above exact multiples. For the octave to sound clean, the upper note must match the slightly sharp overtone of the lower one. So tuners widen the octaves a little, and the top of a piano ends up about a third of a semitone above where the formula puts it.

^ "stiffness raises its overtones above exact multiples" gloss define: the effect is called inharmonicity

### Other divisions

Twelve is a compromise among small numbers. Thirty-one equal steps give almost exactly quarter-comma meantone and were proposed by Christiaan Huygens. Fifty-three give nearly pure fifths and thirds together. Most of the world's music uses neither twelve steps nor chords, and has never had this problem.
