# The Antikythera mechanism

In 1901 sponge divers brought up a corroded lump of bronze from a Roman-era shipwreck off the Greek island of Antikythera. It turned out to be a geared calculator, built about two thousand years ago, that showed the positions of the Sun and Moon, the phase of the Moon, the dates of eclipses and the year of the Olympic Games. Nothing of comparable mechanical complexity is known from the following thousand years.

## The find

The mechanism survives as eighty-two fragments, holding thirty gear wheels, from a ship that sank around 60 BCE. For half a century nobody could see what it was. Understanding it has depended at each stage on a new way of looking inside the corroded metal.

### The wreck

A storm drove the divers to shelter at Antikythera in 1900, and one of them, surfacing in alarm, reported a heap of corpses and horses on the seabed. They were bronze and marble statues. The ship had been carrying Greek luxury goods westward, probably to Italy. Coins and pottery from the cargo date the sinking to within a decade or two of 60 BCE.

### The fragments

The lump split as it dried in the museum, and in 1902 the archaeologist Valerios Stais noticed a gear wheel in the break. The largest fragment is the size of a paperback. The original device was about the size of a shoebox, with bronze plates and gears in a wooden case. Much of it is missing, either never recovered or lost on the seabed.

^ "Much of it is missing" note elaborate: About a third of the mechanism is thought to survive. All of the gearing for the planets is gone, and its reconstruction rests on the inscriptions and on fittings on the surviving main wheel.

### Seeing inside

In the 1950s Derek de Solla Price began a study that ended with gamma-ray images showing gears stacked in layers. Michael Wright, a curator at the Science Museum in London, later built his own X-ray apparatus to separate the layers. In 2005 a team brought an eight-tonne X-ray scanner to Athens and produced three-dimensional images in which teeth can be counted and thousands of characters of hidden text read.

^ "thousands of characters of hidden text" note elaborate: The letters are about two millimetres high. They amount to a user's guide on the cover plates, labels on the dials, and lists of what each display shows.

## What it showed

A handle on the side moved a date pointer round the front dial, and everything else followed through the gears. The front showed the sky: Sun, Moon and probably the five planets against the zodiac. The back held two large spiral dials that tracked calendars and eclipses.

### The front dial

Two rings ran round the front, one for the zodiac and one for the Egyptian calendar of 365 days, with pointers for the date and for the Sun and Moon. A small ball, half silver and half black, turned to show the Moon's phase. An inscription on the front lists the risings and settings of stars through the year.

^ "turned to show the Moon's phase" note elaborate: The ball was driven by the difference between the Moon's motion and the Sun's, which is exactly what phase is. A small gear set at right angles on the Moon's pointer subtracted one from the other.

### The calendar spiral

The upper back dial was a spiral of 235 cells, one per lunar month. That number is the Metonic cycle: 235 lunar months come to almost exactly nineteen years, so the dial kept a calendar of lunar months in step with the seasons. The month names are those of Corinth or one of its colonies. A small dial inside counted the four-year cycle of the games, with Olympia and others named.

^ "235 lunar months come to almost exactly nineteen years" note elaborate: The two differ by about two hours. Nineteen years is 6,939.6 days and 235 months is 6,939.7. The Babylonians used the cycle for their calendar, and the Hebrew calendar still does.

### The eclipse spiral

The lower back dial had 223 cells, the number of months after which eclipses repeat. Marks in particular cells say whether a solar or lunar eclipse may occur in that month and at what hour. The cycle is Babylonian, drawn from centuries of records.

^ "the number of months after which eclipses repeat" gloss define: known as the saros, a little over eighteen years
^ "at what hour" note why: The cycle is eighteen years, eleven days and about eight hours. The odd eight hours shift each repeat a third of the way round the Earth, so a small extra dial shows how many hours to add in each of three successive cycles.

## How it worked

The mechanism multiplies and divides by the ratios of tooth counts. Most of it is simple gear trains. Two parts are far from simple: a device that makes the Moon speed up and slow down as the real Moon does, and the lost machinery that made the planets go backward.

### Gear trains

A wheel of 38 teeth driving one of 64 turns it at 38/64 the speed. To make the Moon pointer go round 254 times while the Sun pointer goes round 19, the builder chained pairs of wheels whose tooth counts multiply to that ratio. The teeth are hand-cut triangles about a millimetre and a half high.

^ "254 times while the Sun pointer goes round 19" note why: In nineteen years there are 235 lunar months, and in each month the Moon gains one full lap on the Sun. Its laps against the stars are therefore 235 plus the Sun's own 19.

### The Moon's uneven speed

The Moon moves faster when closer to the Earth. To imitate this, two gears of equal size sit one on top of the other on axes slightly offset, coupled by a pin on one that rides in a slot on the other. As they turn, the pin slides in and out along the slot and the driven wheel alternately gains and loses on the driver.

^ "a pin on one that rides in a slot on the other" passage elaborate: The pair rides on a larger wheel that itself turns once in about nine years. That is the time the Moon's point of closest approach takes to circle the sky, so the fast part of the Moon's month drifts round the zodiac as it does in reality.

  The effect reproduces the theory of the Moon attributed to Hipparchus, who worked on Rhodes about a century before the wreck. It is that theory cut in bronze.

### The planets

Inscriptions name the planets and give their cycles, and the main wheel carries pillars and bearings for missing parts. Reconstructions by Michael Wright and later by a team at University College London show that the same pin-and-slot idea, mounted on turning plates, can make each planet pause, back up and advance again as it does in the sky. No part of that gearing survives, so this portion is a well-supported inference.

## Who made it

There is no signature. The design draws on Babylonian period relations and Greek geometric theory, and the best clues to where it was made are the month names and the games on its dials. Ancient writers describe similar devices, and their descriptions were long dismissed as exaggeration.

### Clues in the text

The calendar's month names point to Corinth or to a Corinthian colony such as Syracuse, or to Epirus in north-west Greece. The games dial includes a minor festival held on Rhodes. The style of the lettering suggests a date between about 200 and 70 BCE.

^ "between about 200 and 70 BCE" note challenge: One study of the eclipse dial argued that its cycle starts in 205 BCE, which would make the design older than most had assumed. Another puts the start in 178 BCE. The date remains open.

### Candidates

Cicero writes that Archimedes of Syracuse built a bronze device showing the motions of Sun, Moon and planets, carried off to Rome when the city fell in 212 BCE. He also says his own teacher Posidonius, on Rhodes, had recently made one. Hipparchus worked on Rhodes too. The mechanism fits a tradition these reports describe; which workshop made this one is unknown.

^ "Cicero writes" note elaborate: The passages are in *On the Republic* and *On the Nature of the Gods*. Until the mechanism was understood, most scholars took them to describe simple models or to be literary invention.

### Not the first

Its layout is compact and its design confident. Nothing about it looks like a prototype. A workshop that could make this had made others, and the surviving object is one late example of a craft whose other products were lost.

## Why nothing else survives

The gap between this object and the next geared astronomical machines is more than a thousand years. The reasons are ordinary. Bronze is valuable and was melted for reuse, and a device like this had no practical use to preserve it.

### Bronze is recycled

Almost every ancient bronze statue we have comes from a shipwreck or a burial, places where no one could reach it to melt it down. The mechanism survives for the same reason. An instrument that stayed on land would have gone into the furnace when it broke or when its owner died.

### The thread

Knowledge of gearing did not vanish entirely. A Byzantine sundial of about 500 CE contains a small geared calendar, and al-Biruni described a similar one around 1000. Gearing of anything like the mechanism's ambition returns only with the astronomical clocks of the fourteenth century, such as the one Giovanni de' Dondi finished in Padua in 1364.

### What it says about what we know

Before this find, no historian would have credited Hellenistic craftsmen with such a device, and the written sources that hinted at it were discounted. One chance survival overturned the picture. It is a reminder that the record of ancient technology is a small and biased sample.

^ "a small and biased sample" note example: Ancient texts mention water organs, automatic doors, coin-operated dispensers and a steam-driven spinning ball. Not one of those survives as an object, and without the texts we would not guess at them.
