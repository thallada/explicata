# The invention of writing

Writing was invented from nothing only a handful of times: certainly in Mesopotamia and in Mesoamerica, almost certainly in China, and perhaps in Egypt. Everywhere else it was borrowed, or built by people who knew that writing existed. The clearest record of how it happened shows that it began as accounting, and that the step which made it writing was a pun.

## What counts as writing

Writing is a system of marks that records a particular language, so that a reader recovers the words and not just the gist. Pictures, tallies and road signs carry meaning without doing this. The line matters because crossing it is what was hard.

### Signs before writing

A picture of a sheep beside five strokes says five sheep to anyone, in any language. That is its strength and its limit. It cannot say that the sheep were promised, or stolen, or belong to a man called Kushim. Systems of this kind are tens of thousands of years older than writing and still thrive, in airports and on dashboards.

^ "a man called Kushim" note elaborate: The name appears on a group of tablets from Uruk recording barley, dated around 3200 BCE. It is often cited as the earliest personal name known, though it may be a title or an office.

### Recording the words

Full writing can record any sentence of its language, including names, grammar and abstract words. To do that it must, at least in part, represent sounds. Every known writing system does, including those such as Chinese that are often described as picturing ideas.

^ "often described as picturing ideas" note challenge: Around four fifths of Chinese characters are compounds in which one part hints at the meaning and the other at the pronunciation. The script records the Chinese language, syllable by syllable; it is not a universal code of ideas.

## Mesopotamia: from tokens to tablets

In southern Iraq the path can be followed step by step over several thousand years. Small clay counters used for keeping track of goods were sealed in clay envelopes, the envelopes were marked on the outside, and the marks made the contents unnecessary. The earliest tablets are almost all accounts.

### Counters and envelopes

From about 8000 BCE, villages across the Near East used small clay shapes, such as cones, spheres and discs, to stand for measures of grain, jars of oil and animals. By the fourth millennium, sets of them were sealed inside hollow clay balls as a record of a transaction. To show what was inside without breaking the ball, people pressed the tokens into its surface before sealing it.

^ "small clay shapes" note elaborate: The archaeologist Denise Schmandt-Besserat catalogued thousands of them and argued that they are the direct ancestors of the first written signs. The link to the number signs is widely accepted; her claim that most word signs also descend from tokens is not.

### The tablet

Once the outside carries the information, the inside is redundant. A flattened lump of clay with impressed marks does the same job. Around 3300 BCE the temple administrators of Uruk, then perhaps the largest city in the world, were keeping records this way, adding signs scratched with a stylus for the kinds of goods, and soon some 1,200 distinct signs were in use.

^ "Uruk" gloss define: a city on the Euphrates, in what is now southern Iraq

### What the first tablets say

About nine tenths of the early tablets from Uruk are administrative: rations issued, fields measured, animals counted. The rest are lists of words for scribes to copy when learning the signs. There are no stories, letters, laws or prayers for several centuries. Writing was a tool of bookkeeping long before it was anything else.

^ "lists of words for scribes to copy" note elaborate: These lists name professions, vessels, trees, cities. They were copied with little change for more than a thousand years, which is how modern scholars learned what many archaic signs meant.

## The rebus

A set of signs for things cannot write a name or a verb ending. The way out was to use a sign for its sound and ignore its meaning. This is the rebus principle, and it is the moment bookkeeping became writing.

^ "rebus" gloss define: a picture used for the sound of its name, as an eye for the word I

### Sound from pictures

In Sumerian the word for arrow was *ti*, and so was the word for life. A scribe who needed to write life, which cannot be drawn, drew an arrow. Once that is allowed, any word can be spelled out with pictures of things that sound like its parts. The first use was the one administrators needed most: personal names.

^ "drew an arrow" note example: A tablet from Jemdet Nasr, around 3000 BCE, has a name usually read as En-lil-ti, meaning the god Enlil gives life, with the arrow sign standing for the last word.

### Cuneiform grows up

Over the next centuries the signs lost their pictures, becoming clusters of wedges pressed with a cut reed, and gained fixed sound values. By about 2600 BCE scribes were writing whole Sumerian sentences in order, and literature follows. The script was then adapted to Akkadian, a wholly unrelated language, and later to Hittite and others. It stayed in use for three thousand years.

^ "clusters of wedges" note why: Drawing curves in wet clay raises ridges and is slow. Pressing the corner of a reed leaves a clean wedge. The look of the script is a consequence of its medium.

## The other inventions

In China and Mesoamerica writing appears with no plausible outside source, and in Egypt it appears suddenly at about the time of the first Mesopotamian tablets. In every case, mature writing combines signs for words with signs for sounds, which suggests the rebus is less a stroke of genius than the only door.

### Egypt

Small bone and ivory labels from a royal tomb at Abydos, dated around 3250 BCE, carry signs that already use the rebus to write place names. That is as early as the first tablets of Uruk. Egypt was in contact with Mesopotamia then, so the idea may have travelled, but the signs themselves owe nothing to cuneiform.

^ "the idea may have travelled" note define: Scholars call this stimulus diffusion: borrowing the notion that speech can be recorded without borrowing any of the signs. The Cherokee script, devised by Sequoyah in the 1820s, is a documented case.

### China

The oldest Chinese writing known is on ox shoulder blades and turtle shells used for divination at the Shang court, from about 1250 BCE. Questions to the ancestors were carved beside the cracks that answered them. The script is already complete, with thousands of characters and regular use of sound-borrowing, so it must have had earlier stages, probably on bamboo and wood that have not survived.

### Mesoamerica

Writing in Mexico and Guatemala arose with no possible contact with the Old World. The Maya script, the best understood, mixes word signs with a full set of syllable signs, the same design as cuneiform and Egyptian. It was read in the twentieth century once Yuri Knorozov showed, against fierce resistance, that many of its signs spell sounds.

^ "against fierce resistance" note elaborate: The leading Mayanist of the day, Eric Thompson, held that the glyphs recorded ideas and calendar lore, not speech. Knorozov, working in Leningrad from published copies, argued in 1952 that they were syllabic. The field came round only in the 1970s.

## The alphabet

Almost every alphabet in use descends from one invention. Around 1900 to 1800 BCE, speakers of a Semitic language who knew Egyptian writing took a couple of dozen pictures and used each for the first sound of its name. Everything else, from Greek to Arabic to the scripts of India, is adaptation.

### One sign, one sound

The principle is called acrophony. The picture of an ox head stood for the first sound of the Semitic word for ox, *ʾalp*; the picture of a house for the first sound of *bayt*. The earliest examples are scratched on rocks at Wadi el-Hol in Egypt and at turquoise mines in Sinai. Egyptian already had signs for single consonants; the novelty was throwing away everything else.

^ "throwing away everything else" note why: Egyptian scribes never took this step, in three thousand years. A script that takes a decade to learn supports a profession, and the people best placed to simplify it were the ones with most to lose.

### Descent

The Phoenicians carried a tidy form of this script, with twenty-two consonants, around the Mediterranean. Greeks borrowed it around 800 BCE and reused letters for sounds Greek lacked as vowels, giving the first script to write vowels and consonants on equal terms. Aramaic, another descendant, is the ancestor of the Hebrew and Arabic scripts and very probably of the Brahmi script behind the writing systems of India and Southeast Asia.

^ "reused letters for sounds Greek lacked as vowels" note example: The first letter, *ʾalp*, wrote a catch in the throat that Greek does not have. Greeks heard only the vowel that followed it and used the letter for *a*.

## What writing changed

For most of its history few people could read, and writing served states and temples. Its effects were on what institutions could remember: debts, laws, borders and observations kept over spans longer than a life.

### Memory outside the head

A written record can be checked against a claim, and it outlasts the person who made it. Babylonian scholars kept nightly records of the sky for some seven hundred years, the longest continuous scientific record ever made, and found periods in the motions of the Moon and planets that no single lifetime of watching could reveal.

^ "nightly records of the sky" gloss define: known as the Astronomical Diaries, kept from about 650 BCE

### An old complaint

In Plato's *Phaedrus*, Socrates tells of an Egyptian god offering writing to a king as a cure for forgetfulness. The king replies that it will produce forgetfulness instead, since people will trust marks outside themselves and stop exercising their memory. We know the argument because Plato wrote it down.
