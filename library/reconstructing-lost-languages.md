# Reconstructing lost languages

Nobody wrote down the language from which English, Russian, Persian, Hindi, Greek and Welsh all descend. It was spoken some six thousand years ago by people without writing. Yet linguists can state its words for father, wheel and snow, describe its grammar, and say with some confidence where its speakers lived. The method that makes this possible rests on one surprising fact: sounds change with regularity.

## The discovery of a family

In the late eighteenth century European scholars noticed that Sanskrit resembled Greek and Latin too closely for chance. The right explanation, that all three descend from a language no longer spoken, founded historical linguistics.

### The resemblance

In 1786 William Jones, a judge in Calcutta, told a learned society that Sanskrit bore to Greek and Latin an affinity so strong that they must have sprung from some common source which perhaps no longer exists. The observation was not wholly new, but the conclusion was the right one: the three are sisters, and none is the mother.

^ "none is the mother" note why: The natural guess was that the oldest attested language, Sanskrit, was the ancestor. It is not. In some features Sanskrit has changed more than Latin or Lithuanian have.

### What counts as evidence

Similar words prove little. Languages borrow words, and chance supplies lookalikes: English *much* and Spanish *mucho* are unrelated. The evidence that counts is systematic. Where Latin has *p* at the start of a word, English has *f*, again and again, across basic vocabulary and grammar alike.

^ "English *much* and Spanish *mucho* are unrelated" note elaborate: *Much* comes from Old English *micel*, great. *Mucho* comes from Latin *multum*. Their resemblance is an accident of separate histories.

## Sound laws

The founding result is that a sound change affects every word that has the sound in the relevant position. Because change is regular, it can be run backward. The apparent exceptions to the first great sound law turned out to obey a second, and that success made regularity the working assumption of the field.

### Grimm's law

In the ancestor of the Germanic languages a whole series of consonants shifted. Where Latin and Greek kept *p*, *t*, *k*, Germanic has *f*, *th*, *h*. So Latin *pater*, *tres*, *cornu* answer to English *father*, *three*, *horn*. Rasmus Rask set out the pattern in 1818 and Jacob Grimm, of the fairy tales, systematised it in 1822.

^ "a whole series of consonants shifted" passage elaborate: The shift moved three sets of sounds in a chain. The older *p*, *t*, *k* became *f*, *th*, *h*. The older *b*, *d*, *g* moved into the places they had left, becoming *p*, *t*, *k*: Latin *decem* beside English *ten*, Latin *genu* beside *knee*.

  A third set, breathy consonants preserved in Sanskrit as *bh*, *dh*, *gh*, became plain *b*, *d*, *g*: Sanskrit *bhrātar* beside English *brother*.

### Verner's law

Grimm's law had embarrassing exceptions. Latin *pater* and *frater* both have *t*, yet Old English has *fæder* with *d* and *brōþor* with *th*. In 1875 Karl Verner showed that the outcome depended on where the accent had fallen in the parent language, an accent preserved in Sanskrit and lost in Germanic. The exceptions were a second law.

^ "where the accent had fallen" note example: Sanskrit has *pitár*, with the accent after the consonant, and *bhrā́tar*, with the accent before it. The consonant became voiced only when the accent did not immediately precede it.

### Exceptionless

A group of younger scholars at Leipzig drew the moral: sound laws admit no exceptions, and an apparent exception is an undiscovered law, a borrowing, or a word reshaped by analogy with another. As a claim about nature this is too strong. As a rule of method it was decisive, because it forbids explaining away awkward data.

^ "A group of younger scholars at Leipzig" gloss define: known as the Neogrammarians
^ "reshaped by analogy with another" note example: The old plural of *cow* was *kine*, the regular outcome of the sound changes. *Cows* was built afresh on the pattern of other plurals. Analogy tidies grammar; sound change, which is blind to grammar, untidies it.

## The comparative method

Reconstruction lines up related words, tabulates which sound corresponds to which, and posits for each correspondence a single ancestral sound from which every attested outcome follows by plausible changes. The result is a hypothesis that later discoveries can confirm or sink, and in one famous case a discovery confirmed it.

### Correspondence sets

Take the word for father: Sanskrit *pitár*, Greek *patḗr*, Latin *pater*, Gothic *fadar*, Old Irish *athir*. The first consonant gives a set: *p*, *p*, *p*, *f*, nothing. If the same set recurs in other words, as it does, it reflects one sound of the parent language.

^ "nothing" note elaborate: Celtic lost the sound altogether. Old Irish *athir* is *pater* with the *p* gone, and Irish *iasc*, fish, matches Latin *piscis* the same way.

### Choosing the ancestor

For that set the parent sound is reconstructed as *p*. A change from *p* to *f*, or from *p* to nothing by way of *h*, is common in the world's languages; the reverse is rare. Majority also favours it, but direction of change is the stronger argument. Doing this for every set yields a sound system, and from it whole words.

### The asterisk

A reconstructed form is written with a star in front of it. The word for father, with its star, is *ph₂tḗr*. The star says the form is inferred and not attested. Reconstructions are precise about which sounds were distinct from which, and vague about exactly how they were pronounced.

^ "vague about exactly how they were pronounced" note example: In 1868 August Schleicher wrote a short fable about a sheep and some horses in reconstructed Proto-Indo-European. Each generation of scholars has rewritten it, and the versions look strikingly different, because the theory of the sounds kept improving.

### A prediction confirmed

In 1879 Ferdinand de Saussure, aged twenty-one, argued from patterns in the vowels that the parent language must have had consonants that survive in no known daughter. He could say where they had stood and nothing about their sound. In 1927 Jerzy Kuryłowicz pointed out that Hittite, deciphered a decade earlier from clay tablets found in Turkey, has a consonant written *ḫ* in just those positions.

^ "consonants that survive in no known daughter" note define: They are now called laryngeals and written h₁, h₂, h₃. The small h₂ in the reconstructed word for father is one of them.

## Words as evidence

A reconstructed vocabulary describes the world of its speakers. If the parent language had a word for something, its speakers knew the thing. This method places the Proto-Indo-Europeans in time and, less securely, in space.

### What they had words for

There are reconstructed words for cattle, sheep, pigs, dogs and horses, for grain and ploughing, for wool and weaving, for honey and the drink made from it. There are words for snow and for a few trees. There are words for father's kin in detail and mother's kin hardly at all, and a sky god addressed as father.

^ "a sky god addressed as father" note example: Sanskrit *Dyáuṣ pitā́*, Greek *Zeû páter* and Latin *Iūpiter* are the same two words, reconstructed as *dyḗws ph₂tḗr*, sky father.

### Wheels

The parent language had words for wheel, axle, yoke and travelling by vehicle, formed from its own native roots and not borrowed. Wheeled vehicles first appear in the archaeological record around 3500 BCE. The language was therefore still one community after that date, which rules out the theory that it spread with the first farmers three thousand years earlier.

^ "formed from its own native roots" note example: One word for wheel, the source of Greek *kýklos*, Sanskrit *cakrá* and English *wheel*, is a doubled form of the verb to turn: roughly, the turner-turner.

### The steppe

The best-supported homeland is the grassland north of the Black and Caspian Seas, among herders of the Yamnaya culture around 3000 BCE. In 2015 studies of DNA from ancient skeletons showed a massive movement of people with Yamnaya ancestry into Europe at about that time, and related ancestry reaching South Asia later. The linguistic argument was a century older than the genetic evidence that supports it.

^ "The best-supported homeland" note challenge: The oldest branch, the Anatolian languages including Hittite, fits less neatly. People in Anatolia show little steppe ancestry, and some researchers place an earlier stage of the family south of the Caucasus, with the steppe as a secondary homeland for everything else.

## Limits

The method reaches back a limited distance, and it reconstructs a tidier language than anyone ever spoke. Claims to go much deeper have not convinced most linguists.

### The time barrier

Words are replaced, sounds merge, and after enough time the signal of common descent sinks below the level of chance resemblance. Most linguists put the horizon at six to ten thousand years. Proposals of deeper families, such as Nostratic linking Indo-European with Uralic and others, rest on comparisons too few and too loose to tell from accident.

### What a reconstruction is

A reconstructed language has no dialects, no slang and no change over time, though the real one had all three. It is best read as a compact statement of the relationships among the daughters. Its power is that it makes predictions, about what a newly found language or inscription should look like, and that those predictions have come true.
