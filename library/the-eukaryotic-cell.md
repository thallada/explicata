# The one merger

For two billion years life on Earth was single cells of two simple kinds. Then, apparently once, a cell of one kind came to live inside a cell of the other, and their descendants are every animal, plant, fungus and amoeba. The complex cell did not evolve gradually from a simple one. It began as a partnership, and the partner is still there in each of your cells, with its own genes.

## Two kinds of cell

Bacteria and archaea are small cells without internal compartments. Eukaryotes, the cells of everything large, are typically ten thousand times greater in volume and full of internal structure. Nothing alive bridges the gap, and that absence is the puzzle.

^ "archaea" note define: Single-celled organisms that look like bacteria under a microscope but differ from them in their membranes and in the machinery that reads genes. Carl Woese recognised them as a separate domain in 1977 by comparing genetic sequences.

### Simple cells

A bacterium is a bag a few thousandths of a millimetre long with its DNA loose inside. Bacteria and archaea are chemically brilliant: between them they can live on sunlight, hydrogen, iron, sulphur or methane. But in four billion years none has become large and structurally complex. They have stayed small.

^ "They have stayed small" note challenge: A few bacteria are huge. *Thiomargarita magnifica* is a centimetre long. But it is a thin skin of living material around an inert sac, with hundreds of thousands of copies of its genome spread along the skin, which fits the energy argument made later in this document.

### Complex cells

A eukaryotic cell has a nucleus enclosing its DNA, a skeleton of protein filaments, internal membranes that sort and ship materials, and mitochondria that supply its energy. It can swallow other cells whole. Its genome is typically hundreds of times larger than a bacterium's.

### Shared peculiarities

All eukaryotes share the same long list of complex traits: the nucleus, sex, linear chromosomes, the same skeleton proteins, genes interrupted by stretches of meaningless sequence. No living group has half the list. So all of it was in place in a single ancestor before the surviving lines diverged, and whatever intermediate forms existed have left no descendants.

^ "a single ancestor" gloss define: called LECA, the last eukaryotic common ancestor

## The merger

Mitochondria were once free-living bacteria. The cell that took them in was an archaeon. The first claim was ridiculed for decades and is now beyond dispute; the second has become clear only since 2015.

### Mitochondria were bacteria

Mitochondria have their own DNA, a small circle like a bacterial chromosome. They have their own ribosomes, of the bacterial kind, sensitive to some of the same antibiotics. They have two membranes, and they multiply only by dividing. Their gene sequences place them among a known group of bacteria.

^ "a known group of bacteria" gloss define: the alphaproteobacteria, which include many species that live inside other cells
^ "multiply only by dividing" note elaborate: A cell cannot make a mitochondrion from scratch. Every one in your body descends by division from those in the egg you grew from, and so on back in an unbroken line some two billion years long.

### Margulis

Lynn Margulis argued the case in a 1967 paper that was rejected by about fifteen journals before it was published. The idea was older, from Russian botanists at the start of the century, but she assembled the evidence and defended it for a decade against near-universal scepticism. Sequencing of mitochondrial genes in the late 1970s settled it.

^ "defended it for a decade" note challenge: She also argued that the cell's whip-like tails came from a merger with corkscrew-shaped bacteria. No evidence for that has turned up. Being right about one merger does not make every merger right.

### The host

Genes for the cell's information handling, which copy and read DNA, most resemble those of archaea. In 2015 a new group of archaea was identified from DNA in mud near a vent field in the Arctic Ocean. These Asgard archaea carry genes previously thought unique to eukaryotes, including ones for the internal skeleton. On present evidence eukaryotes are a branch within the archaea that acquired a bacterium.

^ "from DNA in mud" note elaborate: At first there were only sequences. In 2020 a Japanese team reported growing one of these organisms after twelve years of effort. It is a tiny cell with long branching arms, living pressed against partner microbes with which it exchanges nutrients.

## Why it mattered: energy

One argument, pressed by Nick Lane and William Martin, holds that mitochondria did more than help: they lifted a limit that keeps bacteria simple. A cell makes energy at membranes, and internal power stations with their own small genomes let a cell enlarge its membrane area and its genome together.

### Power at the membrane

Cells make their energy currency by pumping protons across a membrane and letting them flow back through a turbine-like protein. In bacteria the membrane is the cell's outer boundary. As a cell grows, its surface increases as the square of its width and its volume as the cube, so a large bacterium has little power per unit of contents.

^ "a turbine-like protein" note elaborate: The protein is ATP synthase. Its core physically rotates, at over a hundred turns a second, as protons pass through it, and each turn makes three molecules of the energy carrier ATP.

### Genes on site

Folding the membrane inward would add area, but the machinery in each patch of membrane has to be controlled locally, and that seems to need genes close by. Mitochondria kept a handful of genes for exactly the proteins at the core of energy production and lost the rest. A eukaryotic cell thus runs thousands of small power stations, each with a tiny local genome, and one large central genome supported by them all.

^ "lost the rest" note elaborate: The bacterial ancestor had a few thousand genes. Human mitochondria have 37. Most of the others were not deleted but transferred to the nucleus, and their protein products are shipped back in.

### How strong the argument is

By Lane and Martin's estimate, a eukaryote has thousands of times more energy available per gene than a bacterium, which would pay for large genomes and elaborate structure. Critics answer that bacteria do not seem short of energy and that the cost of extra genes is small. Whether energy was the barrier or merely one advantage is disputed.

## How the rest followed

After the merger came the nucleus, sex and the rest of the shared list. For several of these there are arguments that the bacterial guest was itself the cause.

### The nucleus

Bacterial genes that moved into the host genome brought with them self-splicing parasitic sequences, which spread and became the interruptions found in eukaryotic genes. A cell with interrupted genes must finish cutting the interruptions out of each message before it is translated into protein. One proposal is that the nuclear membrane arose to keep the two steps apart.

^ "the interruptions found in eukaryotic genes" gloss define: called introns

### Sex and death

All eukaryotes have sex or descend from ancestors that did. Mixing genomes may have been a way to cope with a genome under bombardment from incoming bacterial DNA. Mitochondria also hold the trigger for programmed cell death: when a cell is to be dismantled, a protein released from them starts the process.

### Two genomes in step

The proteins of the energy machinery are built partly from nuclear genes and partly from mitochondrial ones, and the parts must fit to within the width of an atom. The two genomes are inherited differently, since mitochondria come almost always from the mother alone, and keeping them matched is a continuing strain. When they are mismatched the result is disease.

^ "from the mother alone" note why: If mitochondria from both parents mixed, variants that multiply faster within a cell at the cell's expense could spread. Inheritance from a single parent keeps all the mitochondria in a cell close kin and removes the opening for that contest.

## Once or often

Complex cells seem to have arisen a single time. Yet one cell taking up residence in another has happened repeatedly since. The difference between those facts says something about what was hard.

### Other mergers

Chloroplasts, where plants carry out photosynthesis, are former cyanobacteria taken up by a eukaryote over a billion years ago. Some algae then were swallowed by other eukaryotes, giving nested cells within cells. An amoeba called *Paulinella* acquired its own photosynthetic bacterium independently, perhaps a hundred million years ago.

### Why only once

All those later mergers had a eukaryote as host, a cell already able to engulf others. The first merger had no such host. Either it was extraordinarily improbable, or it happened more than once and one lineage displaced the rest. The fossil record of single cells is too poor to tell.

^ "Either it was extraordinarily improbable" note elaborate: The question bears on life elsewhere. If simple cells arise readily but complex ones need a freak event, the universe could hold many living worlds and very few with anything larger than a microbe.

### What we are

Each of your cells holds hundreds or thousands of mitochondria, and at rest they turn over roughly your body weight in ATP every day. An organism is usually pictured as one lineage. Every eukaryote is two, bound together so long ago that neither can live apart.
