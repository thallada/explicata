# Octopus minds

The last common ancestor of an octopus and a human was a small worm-like animal that lived more than five hundred million years ago and had, at most, a few thousand neurons. Everything that makes an octopus clever evolved separately from everything that makes a mammal clever. It is the nearest thing we have to meeting an intelligent alien, and its body is organised on a different plan from ours: most of its neurons are in its arms.

## A separate experiment

Large nervous systems evolved at least twice, in vertebrates and in the cephalopod molluscs. The octopus's relatives are clams and snails. Why this one branch grew a big brain seems to come down to losing the shell.

^ "cephalopod" gloss define: the group containing octopuses, squid, cuttlefish and the nautilus

### The common ancestor

The lines leading to molluscs and to vertebrates split before either had eyes of any sophistication or a centralised brain. Any resemblance between an octopus and a vertebrate in vision, learning or play is therefore a case of evolution arriving twice at the same answer. The camera-like eye is the famous example.

^ "The camera-like eye" note elaborate: The octopus eye has a lens, an iris and a retina like ours, built from different tissue. Its wiring runs behind the retina, so it has no blind spot, and it focuses by moving the lens back and forth as a camera does.

### Losing the shell

Early cephalopods lived in shells, as the nautilus still does. The ancestors of octopuses and squid gave the shell up, gaining speed and the ability to squeeze into crevices, and becoming soft, edible and unprotected. An animal like that survives by perception, concealment and wit. The big brain is plausibly the price of nakedness.

### Live fast

Most octopuses live one or two years. They hatch tiny, grow fast, breed once and die. A female guards her eggs without eating and dies about when they hatch. This is strange company for intelligence, which in birds and mammals goes with long lives, parental teaching and society. An octopus learns everything it knows alone and quickly.

^ "dies about when they hatch" note elaborate: The decline is set off by a gland between the eyes. In a 1977 experiment, females with the gland removed abandoned their eggs, resumed feeding and lived months longer.

## A body with many minds

An octopus has about five hundred million neurons, comparable to a dog, and roughly two thirds of them are in the arms. The brain issues broad commands and the arms work out the details. Where the animal's control is centred is not a question with a clean answer.

### Counting neurons

The central brain, wrapped around the oesophagus, holds some forty to fifty million neurons, and the two optic lobes behind the eyes hold a hundred million or more. The rest run down the eight arms in thick nerve cords, with clusters of nerve cells serving each of some two hundred suckers on every arm.

^ "wrapped around the oesophagus" note elaborate: Food passes through the middle of the brain on its way to the stomach. An octopus that swallows something sharp can injure its own brain.

### Arms that decide

An arm cut from the body will still pull away from a pinch, and will grasp food and pass it sucker to sucker toward where the mouth would be. In the intact animal the brain appears to send a command such as *reach* and leave the arm to form the movement. An arm has no joints and can bend anywhere, so its possible movements are infinite, and handing the problem to local circuits may be the only workable design.

^ "leave the arm to form the movement" note example: To bring food to its mouth, an octopus arm forms three temporary bends that act like a shoulder, elbow and wrist. It builds a jointed limb for the occasion and dissolves it afterwards.

### What the brain knows

The arms carry little information back about where they are. An octopus cannot easily tell the shape of an object by touch in the way we can, and it guides tricky arm movements by watching. Yet experiments in which an arm must be steered through a maze to food show the brain can take charge when needed. Control is shared, and the split shifts with the task.

^ "Control is shared" note challenge: How independent the arms are is debated. A popular description says an octopus has nine brains, and that overstates it. Arm cords are connected to one another and to the brain, and recent work shows more central coordination than older accounts allowed.

### Tasting by touch

Each sucker is lined with thousands of chemical receptors. An arm exploring a crevice the animal cannot see into is feeling and tasting at once, and can tell prey from rock without consulting the eyes. The receptors were identified in 2020 as a family unique to cephalopods.

## Skin

Octopuses, cuttlefish and squid change colour and texture in a fraction of a second, under direct nervous control. The skin is a display driven by the brain, used to hide and to signal. The animals that operate it are colour-blind.

### Chromatophores

The skin holds up to millions of small elastic sacs of pigment, each ringed by muscles and wired to the brain. When the muscles pull, the sac spreads into a visible disc; when they relax, it shrinks to a dot. Beneath lie layers of reflecting cells that add white, blue and iridescent sheens. Other muscles raise the skin into bumps and spikes to match the texture of rock or weed.

^ "wired to the brain" note why: Chameleons and flatfish change colour by hormones, which takes seconds to minutes. Direct nerve control is why a cuttlefish can run moving bands of dark across its body several times a second.

### Camouflage

A cuttlefish on a patterned floor does not copy what is beneath it. It selects one of a few general patterns, such as uniform, mottled or boldly blocked, depending on the scale and contrast of what it sees. The patterns work against the visual systems of fish, which are the eyes that matter.

^ "which are the eyes that matter" note example: Placed on a checkerboard, a cuttlefish makes no attempt at squares. It shows a blocked pattern of large light and dark patches, which is its answer to any background of high-contrast objects about the size of its own markings.

### Colour-blind artists

Cephalopod eyes have one kind of light receptor, where ours have three, and in behavioural tests they fail to tell apart colours of equal brightness. Yet their camouflage matches colour well. Proposed explanations include light-sensitive proteins in the skin itself, and an optical trick by which their oddly shaped pupils focus different colours at different depths. Neither is established.

^ "light-sensitive proteins in the skin itself" note elaborate: The same family of proteins used in the eye is present in octopus skin, and isolated skin responds to light. Whether that information is used for colour matching is unknown.

## What they do

Octopuses solve problems, learn quickly, and treat individual people differently. Many famous stories about them are anecdotes, and separating what has been shown under controlled conditions from what has been told at aquariums is a large part of studying them.

### Problems and tools

Octopuses learn to open screw-top jars, find their way through mazes, and remember solutions for weeks. In Indonesia, veined octopuses collect halves of coconut shells, carry them tucked under the body while walking awkwardly on stiffened arms, and later assemble them into a shelter. Carrying an object at a cost now for use later meets the usual definition of tool use.

### Play and individuals

Given an empty bottle in a bare tank, some octopuses blow it repeatedly toward the water inlet and catch it when it drifts back, for many minutes. In one experiment, octopuses were approached each day by one person who fed them and another who prodded them with a bristly stick. Within two weeks they behaved differently toward the two, though both wore identical uniforms.

^ "blow it repeatedly" note challenge: Calling this play is an interpretation. It fits the working definition used for other animals: repeated, apparently purposeless, done when the animal is fed and unstressed. Only some individuals did it.

### Escapes

Aquarium staff tell of octopuses that leave their tanks at night to raid neighbouring ones, squirt water at lights to short them out, and squeeze through any gap wider than their beak, the only hard part of the body. In 2016 an octopus named Inky left his tank in a New Zealand aquarium, crossed the floor and went down a drain that led to the sea.

^ "squirt water at lights" note challenge: Stories like this are widely repeated and thinly documented. They are consistent with what controlled studies show octopuses can do, which is some support, but they were not recorded under conditions that rule out embellishment.

### Sleep

Sleeping octopuses pass through an active phase in which their eyes move, their arms twitch and waves of colour pattern cross the skin. A 2023 study found that brain activity in this phase resembles waking activity, and that the skin patterns shown are the animal's waking displays, replayed. It is tempting to call that dreaming, and nobody knows if it is.

## What it is like

Whether an octopus has experience, and what kind, is not known. The evidence that it feels pain is now good enough to have changed laws. Whether its experience is unified is a question the animal's anatomy raises more sharply than any other.

### Pain

An octopus injured on one arm guards and tends that arm, and avoids a place where it was hurt. In a 2021 experiment, octopuses given a painful injection later shunned the chamber where it happened, and preferred a chamber where they had been given a local anaesthetic. That combination is the standard evidence for felt pain in mammals. The United Kingdom recognised cephalopods as sentient in law in 2022.

^ "preferred a chamber where they had been given a local anaesthetic" note why: A reflex could explain pulling away from harm. It cannot explain seeking out a place because relief was once found there. That requires the injury to have been registered as an ongoing bad state.

### One subject or several

If the arms process so much on their own, is there one stream of experience or a central one with partial outliers? The philosopher Peter Godfrey-Smith suggests the likeliest answer is a single subject whose arms are partly self and partly other, attended to at some times and left to themselves at others. We have no way yet to test it.

^ "partly self and partly other" note example: Human bodies have a mild version. You do not direct the muscles that keep you balanced, and you cannot say how you form a word. But for us those processes report to a centre in a way an octopus arm may not.

### Why it matters

Until we have studied a mind built on a different plan, we cannot tell which features of our own are necessary and which are accidents of ancestry. The octopus shows that learning, flexible problem solving and probably feeling can arise without a backbone, a cortex or a long childhood. It widens the range of what a mind might be.
