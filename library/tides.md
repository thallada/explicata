# The tides

The sea rises and falls twice a day because the Moon pulls harder on the near side of the Earth than on the far side. That sentence is true, and nearly everything people infer from it about real tides is wrong. Actual tides are the sloshing of ocean basins driven at fixed rhythms, and the same force that moves the water is slowly lengthening the day and pushing the Moon away.

## The force

Tides are caused by the difference in the Moon's pull across the width of the Earth, not by the pull itself. The difference stretches the planet along the line to the Moon and squeezes it at right angles. The Sun does the same at a little under half the strength.

### Why two bulges

The Earth as a whole falls toward the Moon at the rate set by the pull at its centre. Water on the near side is pulled a little harder than that and gets ahead; water on the far side is pulled a little less and is left behind. Seen from the Earth, both look like bulges pointing away from the centre. The far bulge is not caused by anything spinning.

^ "The far bulge is not caused by anything spinning" note challenge: Many textbooks credit centrifugal force from the Earth and Moon circling their common centre. That description can be made to work, but it misleads: the stretch would be the same if the Earth were falling straight toward the Moon without circling at all.

### Why the Moon beats the Sun

The Sun pulls on the Earth about 180 times harder than the Moon does. But the tidal force depends on how fast the pull changes with distance, which falls off as the cube of the distance, and the Sun is 390 times farther away. The Moon's tidal effect comes out a little over twice the Sun's.

^ "falls off as the cube of the distance" note why: Gravity falls as the square of distance. The tide is the difference in gravity between two points a fixed width apart, and taking that difference brings in one more power of distance.

### How small it is

The tidal force changes your weight by about one part in ten million. It cannot lift water against gravity in any direct sense. What it does is push sideways: everywhere except directly under the Moon and directly opposite, the force has a small horizontal part, and water is free to flow horizontally. Tides are water piled up by sideways nudges acting over whole oceans.

^ "one part in ten million" note example: A lake a few kilometres across has a tide of less than a millimetre. There is not enough width for the sideways push to gather water.

## Springs and neaps

Twice a month the Sun and Moon pull along the same line and the tides are large; in between they pull at right angles and the tides are small. Further rhythms come from the Moon's changing distance and its tilt, and together they make the tide at any port a sum of regular cycles.

### The fortnightly cycle

At new and full moon the two stretches add, giving spring tides. At the quarters the Sun's stretch fills in the Moon's squeeze, giving neap tides. The range at springs is typically about twice that at neaps. Full moon works as well as new because the stretch is symmetric: it does not matter which side the Sun is on.

^ "spring tides" gloss define: from the water springing up; nothing to do with the season

### Other rhythms

The Moon's orbit is an ellipse, and at its closest the tidal force is about forty percent stronger than at its farthest. Its orbit is also tilted, so the two daily high tides are usually unequal. The lunar day is 24 hours 50 minutes, which is why high water comes about fifty minutes later each day.

### Predicting by harmonics

Each cycle contributes a pure wave of known period. The height at a port is the sum of a few dozen such waves, and their sizes and timings for that port can be extracted from a year of measurements. In the 1870s William Thomson built a machine of pulleys and cranks that added the waves mechanically and drew a year of tides in a few hours.

^ "a machine of pulleys and cranks" note elaborate: Each crank turned at the speed of one tidal component, and a single wire threaded over all the pulleys summed their motions. Such machines predicted the tides for the Normandy landings and stayed in service into the 1960s.

## What the ocean actually does

If the oceans kept up with the Moon there would be two smooth bulges and a tide of about half a metre everywhere. They cannot keep up. The real tide is a set of waves rotating around points in each basin, and its height at the coast is decided by the shape of the sea floor.

### Why the bulge model fails

To stay under the Moon a bulge would have to cross the equator at about 1,600 kilometres an hour. A wave in water four kilometres deep travels at about 700. Continents are in the way too. So the ocean never reaches the shape the force is asking for, and it responds as any driven system does, with a lag and an amplitude that depend on how near the driving rhythm is to its own natural ones.

^ "A wave in water four kilometres deep travels at about 700" note define: A tide is a wave much longer than the ocean is deep, and the speed of such a wave is the square root of gravity times depth. Tsunamis travel at the same speed for the same reason.

### Amphidromes

The Earth's rotation turns the sloshing into rotation. In each basin the tide circles a point where the water barely moves, like liquid swirling in a tilted cup. High water sweeps around these points once per tidal period, anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere. Maps of the tide show a dozen or so of them, with lines of equal timing radiating out like spokes.

^ "a point where the water barely moves" gloss define: called an amphidromic point

### Resonance

A bay or shelf sea has a natural sloshing period set by its length and depth. If that period is close to twelve and a half hours, the small ocean tide at its mouth is amplified enormously. The Bay of Fundy, at about thirteen hours, has a range of sixteen metres. The Mediterranean, nearly closed off and far from resonance, has a few tens of centimetres.

^ "A bay or shelf sea has a natural sloshing period" note example: Slide a shallow tray of water back and forth. At most rhythms the water just follows; at one particular rhythm it climbs the ends. The tray's length and the depth of water set that rhythm.

## The brake

The tides dissipate energy, and it comes out of the Earth's rotation. The day is getting longer, and the angular momentum the Earth loses goes to the Moon, which moves outward. Both changes have been measured.

### Friction

The Earth turns faster than the Moon orbits, so friction drags the tidal bulge a little ahead of the line to the Moon. The Moon's pull on that offset bulge acts as a brake. The day lengthens by roughly two milliseconds per century.

^ "roughly two milliseconds per century" note elaborate: Records of ancient eclipses give the figure directly. Eclipses seen in Babylon would have been seen far to the west if the day had always had its present length; the accumulated error over 2,500 years is several hours.

### The receding Moon

The offset bulge also pulls the Moon forward along its orbit, which lifts it into a higher one. Reflectors left on the surface by Apollo astronauts and Soviet landers let laser ranging measure the distance to the centimetre: the Moon recedes 3.8 centimetres a year.

^ "3.8 centimetres a year" note challenge: That rate cannot have held for long. Run backward, it puts the Moon at the Earth about 1.5 billion years ago, and the Moon is three times older. The present arrangement of oceans happens to be close to resonance, so the brake is unusually strong now.

### Days in deep time

Corals and shells lay down daily and yearly growth bands. Devonian corals, about 400 million years old, show roughly 400 daily bands per year. The year has not changed, so the day was then about 22 hours.

## Tides elsewhere

The same force works on solid bodies and at every scale. It has stopped the Moon's rotation, it heats the interiors of moons around giant planets, and close to a compact object it tears things apart.

### Locking

The Earth raises a tide in the rock of the Moon, twenty times stronger than the one the Moon raises here. Friction from it long ago slowed the Moon's spin until it matched its orbit, so we always see the same face. Most large moons in the solar system are locked the same way, and Pluto and its moon Charon are locked to each other.

### Heating

Jupiter's moon Io follows a slightly oval orbit, kept oval by regular tugs from its neighbours Europa and Ganymede. The tide Jupiter raises in it rises and falls every orbit, flexing the rock by about a hundred metres. The friction makes Io the most volcanic body known. The same process keeps an ocean liquid under Europa's ice.

^ "kept oval by regular tugs from its neighbours" note elaborate: Io completes four orbits for every two of Europa and one of Ganymede, so the tugs repeat at the same points of the orbit and add up. Without that the tide itself would round the orbit off and the heating would stop.

### Tearing

Inside a certain distance from a planet, the tidal stretch across a moon exceeds the moon's own gravity, and a body held together only by gravity comes apart. Saturn's rings lie inside this limit. Stars that pass too close to the black hole at the centre of a galaxy are pulled into streams, and the flare as the debris falls in can be seen across the universe.

^ "a certain distance from a planet" gloss define: the Roche limit, about two and a half planetary radii for a fluid moon as dense as the planet
